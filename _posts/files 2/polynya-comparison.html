What Are These Polynyas?

Polynyas are persistent areas of open water or thin ice surrounded by thicker sea ice. In the Amundsen Sea sector of West Antarctica, two polynyas receive outsized scientific attention: the Pine Island Polynya (PIP) and the broader Amundsen Sea Polynya (ASP). Despite their geographic proximity, they form through different mechanisms and play different roles in the regional ocean–ice–atmosphere system.

The PIP forms adjacent to the Pine Island Ice Shelf, where warm, modified Circumpolar Deep Water (mCDW) upwells onto the continental shelf, melting ice from below and maintaining open water from sensible heat input. The ASP, centered further west, is driven primarily by persistent katabatic winds draining off the ice sheet, which push newly formed sea ice offshore — a latent-heat mechanism — though sensible heat from mCDW also contributes.

Note on boundaries
There is no single, universally agreed-upon spatial definition for either polynya. Their extents vary by season, by year, and by the ice concentration threshold used to define "open water" (commonly 70% or 75%). The polygons shown on the map below are approximate representations based on typical austral summer extents reported in the literature, not precise satellite-derived boundaries for any specific date.

Why polygons, not pins?
SciSpace's version used point markers for each polynya. But polynyas are spatial features whose extent, shape, and position vary on daily to seasonal timescales. A point marker communicates location but misrepresents the phenomenon. These polygons are still simplified — real polynya boundaries are irregular and shift constantly — but they better convey the spatial nature of the feature.

Data caveat
These monthly values are representative climatological estimates drawn from published literature describing the seasonal cycle in these regions, not direct pixel-level extractions from a specific NSIDC product. The purpose is to illustrate the seasonal pattern and the relative difference between the two polynyas — not to provide values suitable for quantitative analysis. For research-grade data, use the NSIDC Sea Ice Index or the Bootstrap/NASA Team sea ice concentration products directly.

Comparison

The table below summarizes key differences between the two polynyas. Where quantitative values are given, the source is cited. Many of these values carry substantial uncertainty depending on the method, time period, and spatial definition used.

Attribute Pine Island Polynya Amundsen Sea Polynya
Primary formation mechanism Sensible heat — warm mCDW upwelling beneath and near the Pine Island Ice Shelf melts ice from below Latent heat — katabatic winds push sea ice offshore, exposing open water; sensible heat from mCDW also contributes
Approximate summer extent ~3,000–5,000 km² (highly variable) ~20,000–60,000 km² depending on year and definition (Arrigo & van Dijken, 2003)
Typical open season Nov–Mar, with peak openness Dec–Jan Oct–Apr, longer season driven by wind forcing; peak Dec–Feb
Primary productivity Moderate to high; phytoplankton blooms supported by mCDW-sourced iron Among the most productive polynyas in the Southern Ocean; sustained by both wind-driven upwelling and mCDW iron supply (Arrigo et al., 2012)
Ecological significance Important foraging area; linked to ice shelf melt dynamics relevant to sea level rise Major krill habitat; foraging ground for penguins, seals, whales; carbon export region
Trend (recent decades) Ice shelf thinning and grounding line retreat may be altering polynya characteristics (Rignot et al., 2014) Sea ice season shortening in the broader Amundsen Sea region; polynya opening earlier in some years (Stammerjohn et al., 2015)
What SciSpace presented vs. what's here
SciSpace's comparison table presented values without citations, uncertainty ranges, or caveats. The values above are drawn from specific papers (cited inline) and are accompanied by qualifiers ("highly variable," "depending on year and definition") where appropriate. Even so, each row could warrant its own paragraph of methodological context. Tables are useful for quick comparison but inherently compress complexity.
