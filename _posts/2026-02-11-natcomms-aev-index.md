I am thrilled to share that a paper I helped write, **"An Antarctic ecosystem value index to quantify ecological value across trophic levels and over time,"** is now published in *Nature Communications*.

> DuVivier, A.K., Krumhardt, K., Landrum, L.L., Sylvester, Z. et al. (2026). *An Antarctic ecosystem value index to quantify ecological value across trophic levels and over time.* Nature Communications. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-69011-0

This paper is the scientific backbone of a [larger project](/projects/hotspots-in-the-ice) we've been building for several years. Here I'll summarize what we found, why it matters, and how you can explore the results yourself.

---

### The Problem We Set Out to Solve

The Southern Ocean is changing faster than almost anywhere else on Earth. Sea ice is shifting. Productivity is reorganizing. Fisheries pressure is growing. The international bodies responsible for managing Antarctic marine resources, particularly [CCAMLR](https://www.ccamlr.org/), need science that can answer a deceptively simple question: **where is the most ecologically valuable habitat, and will it still be valuable in the future?**

Previous work had examined individual species or single trophic levels. We wanted a more holistic picture that captured ecosystem value from the base of the food web to the top, across space and through time.

---

### What We Built: The Antarctic Ecosystem Value Index

The Antarctic Ecosystem Value (AEV) Index integrates ecosystem information across trophic levels, from phytoplankton to krill and fish, and ultimately to penguins, producing a single spatial measure of ecological value for marine areas around Antarctica.

The key findings were:

**Polynyas are biological hotspots.** Coastal polynyas, persistent areas of open water within the sea ice, have Index values **31–72% higher** than surrounding waters. These are not simply unusual physical features. They are regions where productivity is concentrated and where ice-dependent species consistently aggregate.

**High-value areas are likely to persist.** Earth System Model projections suggest that areas with high Index values today generally remain areas of high ecological value through the end of the century, despite substantial environmental change. This is not uniformly positive news. Instead, it emphasizes the importance of protecting these regions before conditions shift further.

**Penguins provide an early warning signal.** Penguin habitat suitability is already declining in several regions and is projected to decline further. Because penguins integrate multiple aspects of ecosystem condition, including prey availability, sea ice, and breeding habitat, these projected declines provide an important indicator of broader ecosystem change.

**Protection gaps remain.** Many areas with high Index values already fall within existing or proposed marine protected areas, but the analysis also highlights regions that remain underrepresented, particularly **East Antarctica** and the **Amundsen Sea**.

---

### Why This Approach Matters

Building an index that spans multiple trophic levels required deliberate decisions about how fundamentally different datasets should be combined. We developed the Index iteratively, working closely with CCAMLR working groups over several years so that methodological decisions reflected the needs of end users as well as analytical considerations.

The result is both a research product and a decision-support tool. The Index is not intended to replace expert judgement. Rather, it provides a quantitative framework that helps place those decisions into a broader spatial and ecological context.

---

### The Tools Built on This Science

The paper provides the scientific foundation, but we also developed two tools to make the results accessible and useful.

**[The AEV Interactive Map](https://only.one/map/antarctica-ecoindex#3.7/-85/169)** allows users to explore the Index across trophic levels and compare present-day conditions with projections for 2090. The map also includes regional narratives that place the results into ecological context.

**[The Index Comparison Tool](https://saef-monash.shinyapps.io/antarctic_map/)** brings together the AEV Index with other major circumpolar biodiversity analyses and bioregionalizations, allowing users to compare datasets and calculate overlap for any CCAMLR planning domain or existing and proposed marine protected area.

All underlying datasets are openly available at https://zenodo.org/records/16990591.
